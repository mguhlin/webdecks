A district can write a responsible AI policy today and still find parts of its guidance out of date six months from now. I think the response is to keep our values steady while improving how we act on those values. This session is about giving school leaders a practical way to do both. We will look at the policies that should endure, the procedures that need to change, and the evidence that tells us whether new tools are helping. I will also show how six frameworks fit together without turning them into six more forms everyone has to fill out.

I recommend separating district governance into three layers. The first is enduring policy: our commitments to privacy, safety, academic integrity, accessibility, and human oversight. The second is operational guidance, where vendor reviews, approval criteria, permissions, and procedures live. The third is classroom guidance that teachers and students can use. These layers change at different speeds. A quarterly operational review is a suggested cadence, not a legal requirement. Major changes should trigger a review sooner. TeachAI makes a similar distinction between established policies and more flexible guidance, while recognizing that district processes and legal review still matter.

The Five Phases of School AI Adoption from TCEA provide a language for describing where a district stands. The phases are AI Secured, AI Exploration, AI Pilot Programs, AI Unification, and AI Everywhere. The original model also describes five categories: district policy, account management, guidance, curriculum integration, and community outreach. A district may be in different places on different dimensions. The aim is not to race toward AI Everywhere or to approve every new tool. The aim is to understand what the organization can currently support, what safeguards are missing, and which next step has the most educational value.

Section 1

Give people room to solve problems.

Clear boundaries can make small, evidence-producing experiments possible.
✦ AI Policies That Don’t ExpireJ. Miguel Guhlin · TCEAmguhlin.org

One of the things Gen AI changes is how quickly a small group can test an idea. I have sat in meetings where we are still discussing whether to solve a problem while someone has already built a rough prototype. That energy can be useful in schools, but it needs thoughtful limits. We cannot experiment with actual student records in unapproved systems, and a prototype is not the same as a district-ready product. Within those boundaries, though, teachers and instructional coaches should be able to investigate real instructional problems and bring evidence back to district leaders. That is the next part of the conversation.

Imagine a seventh-grade teacher responsible for reading a large number of essays. The teacher and an instructional coach wonder whether an approved AI assistant could suggest useful next-step feedback. Rather than rushing into live student submissions, they test the idea with fictional essays and compare the output with their own professional feedback. They look for inaccuracies, generic comments, time saved, and whether the suggestions would help students revise. A district leader can support that kind of trial with a narrow purpose and clear boundaries. The point is to learn what works before expanding access or changing classroom procedures.

When I talk about reviews, I do not mean asking whether we should add another paragraph to the handbook. I want to see what educators tried. Suppose three teachers each test a different way to create differentiated reading materials. One method is fast but produces errors. Another is reliable but adds preparation time. A third helps the teachers get to usable drafts that they can verify quickly. The third may merit a supervised expansion. The others are not failures to hide; they tell us where not to invest more effort. Quarterly is a useful starting point, but the evidence and the risks should determine the next decision.

✦ AI Policies That Don’t ExpireJ. Miguel Guhlin · TCEAmguhlin.org

SHINE is a TCEA framework for examining whether a technology belongs in teaching and learning. The letters stand for Suitability, Human Oversight, Insight into Data, Necessity and Novelty, and Ethics and Equity. Those terms matter because a vendor demo usually emphasizes what a tool can do. We need to ask whether it fits our curriculum, whether teachers maintain control, what data it depends on, whether the new approach improves an actual task, and who may be left out or harmed. A tool can satisfy all the technical requirements and still be unnecessary. SHINE helps instructional, procurement, and technology staff ask the same questions.

The PROTECT rubric adds a focused student privacy and AI safety lens. It covers parental rights and access, retention and deletion, opt-out options, transparency, encryption and security, consent and age restrictions, and third-party management. A district can use it to organize vendor questions and identify missing evidence. I would not treat a rubric score or an AI-generated summary of terms as proof that a vendor complies with law. Technology leaders and counsel still need to review the actual agreements and technical controls. This is especially important when the vendor changes data collection, retention, subprocessors, or an AI feature after its initial approval.

Consider a learning management system the district already uses. A product update introduces AI summaries of students' discussions. The district has already approved the vendor, but not necessarily this capability. The policy commitments to privacy, transparency, safety, and human oversight remain relevant. The operational team needs to examine what student content is used, where it goes, how long it is retained, and whether teachers can disable the feature. The district may update permissions, contracts, procedures, and the approved-feature list. There is no automatic need to rewrite board policy, though a legal or policy gap could still require formal revision.

✦ AI Policies That Don’t ExpireJ. Miguel Guhlin · TCEAmguhlin.org

A community can have serious differences over AI use and still agree on educational commitments. Some families want more support for students who need it, while others worry about dependence, privacy, and academic integrity. I would put realistic scenarios in front of them instead of asking whether they are for or against AI. Is it acceptable for AI to explain a math concept? To submit an answer? To ask questions as the learner works? Include students, families, educators, and technology staff, and ask them to explain their reasoning. Participation should influence actual guidance, and leaders should explain where safeguards or legal requirements limit the choices.

Section 2

Keep student learning at the center.

A better-looking artifact is not the same as deeper understanding.
✦ AI Policies That Don’t ExpireJ. Miguel Guhlin · TCEAmguhlin.org

Tools can generate polished assignments and convincing explanations. That makes it especially important to decide what evidence of learning we want from students. A beautiful product is worth examining, but it is not sufficient proof that a student understands a concept. I want to connect three research-informed resources here. Visible Learning helps us think about instructional strategies. SOLO helps us describe the structure of student understanding. ACE offers a short, practical way to ask students to articulate, connect, and extend what they know. These frameworks complement technology reviews. They help us examine the educational outcome after the technical and privacy questions have been addressed.

Professional learning cannot be a one-time demonstration of new chatbot features. Teachers need to examine problems they recognize, test appropriate uses of technology, and share what happened. Imagine a science teacher who receives a polished ecosystems worksheet from a generative AI tool. It is attractive, but almost every question asks for factual recall. With an instructional coach, the teacher redesigns the experience to require explanation and application. The revised lesson becomes a discussion point in professional learning, and the insights inform district classroom guidance. When we do this routinely, professional learning and policy improvement reinforce each other.

✦ AI Policies That Don’t ExpireJ. Miguel Guhlin · TCEAmguhlin.org

SOLO stands for Structure of the Observed Learning Outcome. It helps us describe the complexity of a learner's response. At the unistructural level, a student can name one relevant fact. At the multistructural level, the student knows several facts but has not connected them. Relational understanding appears when the learner explains why those ideas fit together. Extended abstract understanding is evident when a learner generalizes or applies the idea in a new context. I use SOLO to examine the evidence in student work, not to label students. An AI-generated report can look advanced even when the learner's own explanation is still at the surface level.

✦ AI Policies That Don’t ExpireJ. Miguel Guhlin · TCEAmguhlin.org

ACE is short enough to become a routine. Articulate asks a student to explain an idea in their own words. Connect asks how the idea relates to something already known, ideally with an explanation of why the connection works. Extend asks the learner to use it in a new context or generate a testable question. These moves connect naturally to SOLO's surface, deep, and transfer progression. They are useful whether AI helped create the work or not. When I see an unusually polished assignment, I would rather ask students to ACE the underlying concept than assume the artifact alone proves or disproves learning.

Sound bite

An impressive artifact is not always evidence of impressive understanding.

✦ AI Policies That Don’t ExpireJ. Miguel Guhlin · TCEAmguhlin.org

Here is a classroom challenge. Students submit strong-looking science reports with accurate vocabulary, neat diagrams, and polished paragraphs. Several have used AI appropriately, according to the assignment expectations. But during a quick conference, some students cannot explain the relationships described in their own reports. That is useful formative evidence. The teacher can ask an Articulate question, follow with a Connect question about the evidence, and then offer an Extend task in a fresh setting. SOLO helps describe what the responses reveal. This should guide scaffolding and instruction rather than become a gotcha exercise to accuse students of cheating.

Principle 6 · New capabilities

Yesterday’s chatbot answered. Agents can act.

An approved parent platform adds a message-sending AI agent.

Before enabling

  • Map data and system permissions
  • Require appropriate human approval
  • Review vendor terms and audit logs

Do not assume

  • Old approval covers new actions
  • Automation is easy to reverse
  • Every role should get the same access
Decision gate

Approve, restrict, or disable the feature after a targeted review.

✦ AI Policies That Don’t ExpireJ. Miguel Guhlin · TCEAmguhlin.org

Suppose the district's existing parent communication platform introduces an AI agent that can access student information, draft family messages, and possibly send them. No new purchase order is needed because the vendor is already on contract. That is exactly why feature-level review matters. We need to know what systems the agent can access, what information it can retrieve, what actions it can take, who approves those actions, and whether there are logs and reliable ways to stop or reverse them. The response might be approval with conditions, a restricted pilot, or disabling the feature. The key is that capability changes trigger governance decisions.

✦ AI Policies That Don’t ExpireJ. Miguel Guhlin · TCEAmguhlin.org

Here is the practical routine I would recommend. Identify a real problem or a new risk. Authorize an appropriately bounded experiment if it is safe and useful. Evaluate outcomes, including instructional value, privacy, workload, accessibility, and cost. Update the operational guidance or change a tool's approval status, then communicate what people need to do. Repeat the process. A quarterly review works as a starting point, but material vendor changes and new risks should not wait until the calendar meeting. Someone needs to own this process, with representatives from instruction, technology, procurement, leadership, and the school community contributing at the right points.

I would not end this conversation by asking districts to draft another elaborate plan. Start with one operational guideline that has become outdated. Choose one real problem that an approved tool might help solve and define the evidence you would need to justify continued use. Name the person who will bring that information to the next review, and make sure the staff who need the guidance know where to find it. This is how schools can be careful without becoming frozen in place. We can hold to enduring educational values and still make informed changes as technology and evidence evolve.
