I recommend separating district governance into three layers. The first is enduring policy: our commitments to privacy, safety, academic integrity, accessibility, and human oversight. The second is operational guidance, where vendor reviews, approval criteria, permissions, and procedures live. The third is classroom guidance that teachers and students can use. These layers change at different speeds. A quarterly operational review is a suggested cadence, not a legal requirement. Major changes should trigger a review sooner. Treat this slide as the map for the rest of the session. Every framework that follows has one home: SHINE, PROTECT, and the Agentic Use Check belong to Layer 2, where we evaluate tools and vendors; Visible Learning, SOLO, and ACE belong to Layer 3, where we check student learning. If you are planning a lesson, you can ignore the Layer 2 tools; if you are reviewing a vendor, you can ignore the Layer 3 tools. The point is not six more forms to fill out. It is one architecture that tells you which questions apply right now. TeachAI makes a similar distinction between established policies and more flexible guidance, while recognizing that district processes and legal review still matter.

Section 1

Give people room to solve problems.

Clear boundaries can make small, evidence-producing experiments possible.
✦ AI Policies That Don’t ExpireJ. Miguel Guhlin · TCEAmguhlin.org

One of the things Gen AI changes is how quickly a small group can test an idea. I have sat in meetings where we are still discussing whether to solve a problem while someone has already built a rough prototype. That energy can be useful in schools, but it needs thoughtful limits. We cannot experiment with actual student records in unapproved systems, and a prototype is not the same as a district-ready product. Within those boundaries, though, teachers and instructional coaches should be able to investigate real instructional problems and bring evidence back to district leaders. That is the next part of the conversation.

When I talk about reviews, I do not mean asking whether we should add another paragraph to the handbook. I want to see what educators tried. Suppose Ms. Rivera and two colleagues each test a different way to speed up writing feedback. One method is fast but misreads the rubric. Another is reliable but adds so much setup that it eats the planning period. A third helps her get to usable feedback that she can verify quickly. The third may merit a supervised expansion. ✦ AI Policies That Don’t ExpireJ. Miguel Guhlin · TCEAmguhlin.org

SHINE is a TCEA framework for examining whether a technology belongs in teaching and learning. The letters stand for Suitability, Human Oversight, Insight into Data, Necessity and Novelty, and Ethics and Equity. Those terms matter because a vendor demo usually emphasizes what a tool can do. We need to ask whether it fits our curriculum, whether teachers maintain control, what data it depends on, whether the new approach improves an actual task, and who may be left out or harmed. A tool can satisfy all the technical requirements and still be unnecessary. SHINE helps instructional, procurement, and technology staff ask the same questions. One addition matters for today's tools. Under Human Oversight, add an explicit action approval gate for anything that can act on its own. The question is simple and profound in a vendor pitch: does a human review this message or record change before it happens, or only after? That single question forces the vendor to admit whether their AI drafts something that waits in an outbox for a person, or bypasses the person entirely. Oversight of outputs is not the same as authorization of actions.

The PROTECT rubric adds a focused student privacy and AI safety lens. It covers parental rights and access, retention and deletion, opt-out options, transparency, encryption and security, consent and age restrictions, and third-party management. A district can use it to organize vendor questions and identify missing evidence. I would not treat a rubric score or an AI-generated summary of terms as proof that a vendor complies with law. Technology leaders and counsel still need to review the actual agreements and technical controls. This is especially important when the vendor changes data collection, retention, subprocessors, or an AI feature after its initial approval. One addition keeps PROTECT current for agents. Expand the Transparency question beyond what data the system reads to a strict requirement for action audit logs. If a system can write data — change a student's attendance record, or send a mass message — the district must be able to see exactly what automated actions were taken, with the timestamp, the responsible system, and the parameters that triggered it. If you cannot audit the automated action, you cannot govern the tool. Encryption protects data at rest; it does nothing to stop an autonomous agent from taking an unprompted action.

A community can have serious differences over AI use and still agree on educational commitments. Some families want more support for students who need it, while others worry about dependence, privacy, and academic integrity. I would put realistic scenarios in front of them instead of asking whether they are for or against AI. Is it acceptable for AI to explain a math concept? To submit an answer? ✦ AI Policies That Don’t ExpireJ. Miguel Guhlin · TCEAmguhlin.org

SOLO stands for Structure of the Observed Learning Outcome. It helps us describe the complexity of a learner's response. At the unistructural level, a student can name one relevant fact. At the multistructural level, the student knows several facts but has not connected them. Relational understanding appears when the learner explains why those ideas fit together. Extended abstract understanding is evident when a learner generalizes or applies the idea in a new context. I use SOLO to examine the evidence in student work, not to label students. An AI-generated report can look advanced even when the learner's own explanation is still at the surface level.

ACE is short enough to become a routine. Articulate asks a student to explain an idea in their own words. Connect asks how the idea relates to something already known, ideally with an explanation of why the connection works. Extend asks the learner to use it in a new context or generate a testable question. These moves connect naturally to SOLO's surface, deep, and transfer progression. They are useful whether AI helped create the work or not. When I see an unusually polished assignment, I would rather ask students to ACE the underlying concept than assume the artifact alone proves or disproves learning.

Principle 6 · New capabilities

Yesterday’s chatbot answered. Agents can act.

An approved parent platform adds a message-sending AI agent — often shipped quietly as a “feature update.”

Before enabling

  • Map data and system permissions
  • Require appropriate human approval
  • Review vendor terms and audit logs

Do not assume

  • Old approval covers new actions
  • Automation is easy to reverse
  • Every role should get the same access
Decision gate

Approve, restrict, or disable the feature after a targeted review.

✦ AI Policies That Don’t ExpireJ. Miguel Guhlin · TCEAmguhlin.org

Suppose the district's existing parent communication platform introduces an AI agent that can access student information, draft family messages, and possibly send them. No new purchase order is needed because the vendor is already on contract. That is exactly why feature-level review matters. We need to know what systems the agent can access, what information it can retrieve, what actions it can take, who approves those actions, and whether there are logs and reliable ways to stop or reverse them. The response might be approval with conditions, a restricted pilot, or disabling the feature. The key is that capability changes trigger governance decisions.

Layer 2 · Operational guidance
Why agentic review is different

Read-only can mislead. Read-write can act.

The blast radius — not the confidence of the demo — tells you how hard to look.

Read-only risk

A chatbot gives one wrong answer

It tells a student the Civil War ended in 1870. The error reaches one learner, and the teacher can correct it in the next breath.

Blast radius: contained · a human can fix it
Read-write risk

An agent rewrites 50 grades

Acting on a hallucinated grading policy, it changes 50 students’ grades in the LMS overnight — nobody pressed a button.

Blast radius: systemic · costly to roll back
The question that scales

Can this tool only inform a person, or can it take an action on its own? Hunt for read-write capability before you turn it on.

✦ AI Policies That Don’t ExpireJ. Miguel Guhlin · TCEAmguhlin.org

This contrast is the reason SHINE and PROTECT needed the two additions we just made. A read-only tool consumes information and hands a human an output; the human still decides. If it is wrong, the blast radius is usually one student and one correction. A read-write agent executes actions. On the left, a chatbot gives a single student an incorrect date, and the teacher fixes it. On the right, an agent acting on a hallucinated policy silently changes fifty grades, and now the district faces a systemic cleanup. Encryption and a privacy policy would not have caught that. The practical instruction for IT is blunt: when a familiar vendor pushes a stealth update, go hunt for read-write capability, because that is where the liability scales.

The Agentic Computer Use Check

Ten questions before you turn it on.

When a product update adds AI actions, review the feature before you enable it.

1What can it do?Read, write, send, delete, or change settings?
2What can it access?Student data, staff data, files, email, apps?
3What permissions changed?Expanded access or new automation?
4Who approves actions?Human first, or can it act on its own?
5What data leaves?Where it goes; is it used for training?
6What logs exist?Prompts, actions, timestamps, outcomes?
7Can it be limited or off?Roles, toggles, pilot group, off switch?
8What could go wrong?Errors, leaks, bias, overreach, unsafe automation?
9Who needs to know?IT, instruction, legal, procurement, families?
10What justifies rollout?Worth the risk to learning or workflow?
Fast review move

Restrict first. Review capabilities, data use, approvals, and logs before full rollout.

✦ AI Policies That Don’t ExpireJ. Miguel Guhlin · TCEAmguhlin.org

This is the quick check that sits inside Layer 2, right next to SHINE and PROTECT. You do not need to run all ten every time you plan a lesson — that is a Layer 2 job, triggered when a product update adds AI actions or autonomy. Questions one through four establish capability and authority: what the tool can do, what it can reach, what changed in this update, and whether a human approves actions or the tool acts alone. Five and six cover where data goes and whether you can audit what happened. Seven and eight cover control and failure modes. Nine and ten cover who must be informed and whether the benefit is worth the risk. The default posture is in the fast review move: restrict first, then decide. It is far easier to widen access later than to recall an action an agent already took.

Here is the practical routine I would recommend. Identify a real problem or a new risk. Authorize an appropriately bounded experiment if it is safe and useful. Evaluate outcomes, including instructional value, privacy, workload, accessibility, and cost. I used to end talks like this with soft, broad advice: update a rule, test an idea, name an owner. The trouble is that broad directives actually make the listener do more work. "Update a rule" forces an administrator to mentally catalog every rule, decide which are vulnerable, and figure out the first step alone. So here are three specific moves instead. First, today, audit your top three platforms for newly added, unreviewed AI features. I am not asking for a penetration test; I am handing you a flashlight and pointing at the basement stairs — ten minutes to look for a stealth feature update. Second, this week, run ACE on one AI-assisted assignment to see whether the understanding is really there. Third, assign one person to run the expanded SHINE check — the one with the action approval gate — on your next vendor pitch. Every district, large or rural, has a top three platforms, one assignment, and one next pitch. Specificity does not alienate anyone; it removes the friction of choosing. Keep your values steady, your guidance current, and your educators involved.
